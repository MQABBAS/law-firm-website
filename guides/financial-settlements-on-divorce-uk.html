Financial Settlements on Divorce: What UK Couples Need to Know

9 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales

Divorce ends a marriage; it doesn't, by itself, divide anyone's money. Sorting out finances is a separate legal process, and it's one of the areas where getting it wrong — or skipping it — can cause serious problems years down the line. Here's how it actually works.

There's no fixed 50/50 formula

Unlike some jurisdictions, England and Wales don't apply an automatic equal-split rule. The court's overarching duty is to reach a fair outcome, and while an equal division of matrimonial assets is often the starting point — particularly after a long marriage — the court has wide discretion to depart from that based on the specific circumstances of the case.

What the court considers

Section 25 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973 sets out the factors a court weighs, giving first consideration to the welfare of any children under 18. Other factors include:

  • The income, earning capacity, property and other financial resources each party has or is likely to have
  • The needs, obligations and responsibilities of each party
  • The standard of living enjoyed during the marriage
  • The age of each party and the length of the marriage
  • Any physical or mental disability
  • Contributions each party has made, including as a homemaker or carer

What's included

Financial settlements can cover the family home and any other property, savings and investments, business interests, and pensions — which are often overlooked but can be one of the largest assets in a marriage. Pensions can be dealt with by pension sharing (splitting the pension itself), pension offsetting (balancing pension value against other assets like the house), or less commonly, pension attachment orders.

Don't skip finances just because you're amicable

Even where a couple agrees everything informally, without a court-approved financial consent order the agreement isn't legally binding. Either party can potentially bring a claim against the other's finances later — sometimes years afterwards, and even after remarriage in some circumstances.

Financial consent orders

Where a couple reaches agreement — whether directly, through solicitor negotiation, or via mediation — the terms are usually drawn up as a financial consent order and submitted to the court for approval. The court reviews it to check it's broadly fair before making it a binding legal order. Where agreement isn't possible, either party can apply to court for a financial remedy order, which follows a structured court process including financial disclosure from both sides.

Clean break vs ongoing maintenance

Many settlements aim for a clean break — a final order that closes off all future financial claims between the parties, giving both certainty to move on. This isn't always appropriate, though; where there's a significant income disparity or young children, spousal maintenance (ongoing payments from one party to the other) may be ordered instead of, or alongside, a lump sum or asset transfer.
