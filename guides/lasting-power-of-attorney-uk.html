Lasting Power of Attorney: A UK Guide

7 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales

Most people plan carefully for what happens to their money and belongings after death, but far fewer plan for what happens if they lose the ability to manage their own affairs while still alive. A Lasting Power of Attorney fills exactly that gap — and it can only be set up while you still have the capacity to do so.

The two types of LPA

There are two separate types of Lasting Power of Attorney, and it's common — and often sensible — to set up both:

  • Property and Financial Affairs LPA — covers decisions about money, bank accounts, bills, and property. Unlike the health and welfare version, you can choose to let your attorney use this one even while you still have full mental capacity, which can be practically useful (for example, if you're abroad or unwell short-term).
  • Health and Welfare LPA — covers decisions about medical treatment, daily care, and where you live. This one can only be used once you've actually lost the mental capacity to make those decisions yourself.

Why timing matters: capacity

This is the single most important point about LPAs: you must have mental capacity at the time you make one. An LPA cannot be created after capacity has already been lost — at that point, the only route is a deputyship application to the Court of Protection, which is considerably more expensive, slower, and gives the court (not you) more control over who's appointed and how they can act.

An LPA isn't just for later life

Loss of capacity can result from an accident, stroke, or sudden illness at any age — not just dementia in old age. Setting up an LPA is often thought of as something to do "eventually," but it genuinely can't be arranged after the point it's actually needed.

Choosing your attorneys

You can appoint one or more attorneys — commonly a spouse, adult child, or trusted friend — and specify how they must act if there's more than one: jointly (all attorneys must agree on every decision) or jointly and severally (each can act independently). Joint-only appointments offer more oversight but can be impractical if attorneys live apart or disagree; jointly and severally is more flexible but relies more heavily on trust. You can also name replacement attorneys in case your first choice becomes unable or unwilling to act.

Registering an LPA

An LPA must be registered with the Office of the Public Guardian before it can be used — this applies even to a property and finance LPA you intend to let your attorney use straight away. Registration can happen at the same time you set up the LPA, or later, but registering early (rather than waiting until it's urgently needed) avoids delay at a moment when things may already be stressful.

What happens without one

Without a valid, registered LPA, if you lose capacity, nobody — not even a spouse — automatically has authority to manage your finances or make welfare decisions on your behalf. Family members would need to apply to the Court of Protection to become a deputy, a process that takes considerably longer than setting up an LPA in advance, costs more, and results in more ongoing court oversight of decisions than an LPA requires.

This guide describes the general LPA framework in England & Wales as at the date above and is for general information only — it is not legal advice. Scotland has a different system (Power of Attorney under Scots law). Get in touch if you'd like help setting up an LPA.
