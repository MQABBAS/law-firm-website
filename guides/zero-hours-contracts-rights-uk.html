Zero-Hours Contracts: What Rights Do You Actually Have?

6 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales

Zero-hours contracts get a lot of bad press, and some of it is deserved — but they're not the legal free-for-all many people assume. Even without guaranteed hours, most people working under one still have a solid set of statutory rights that employers are required to respect.

What is a zero-hours contract?

A zero-hours contract is one where the employer isn't obliged to offer any minimum number of hours, and the individual isn't obliged to accept work when it's offered. That mutual lack of obligation is the defining feature, and it's also what usually determines the person's legal status — most people on genuine zero-hours arrangements are classed as workers rather than employees, a distinction that affects which rights apply.

Worker status and why it matters

UK employment law recognises different categories of working people, each with a different bundle of rights. Employees get the fullest set of protections, including rights like unfair dismissal and statutory redundancy pay (usually after a minimum period of continuous service). Workers — the category most zero-hours staff fall into — get a narrower but still meaningful set of rights, including the national minimum wage, paid holiday, rest breaks, and protection from unlawful discrimination. Genuinely self-employed contractors get the least statutory protection, but employers sometimes wrongly label people as self-employed or "casual" when their actual working arrangement makes them a worker or even an employee in law — a mislabel that can be successfully challenged.

Your actual working relationship matters more than your contract's label

Employment tribunals look past what a contract calls someone and examine how the relationship actually functions in practice — for example, how much control the employer exercises, whether there's an expectation of regular work, and whether the individual can genuinely turn down shifts without penalty. A written "zero-hours" or "self-employed" label doesn't settle the question if the reality is different.

Rights you're entitled to

As a worker, zero-hours staff are generally entitled to the National Minimum Wage or National Living Wage for all hours actually worked, statutory paid holiday (usually calculated at 12.07% of hours worked, or via rolled-up holiday pay added to each payslip under current rules), rest breaks under the Working Time Regulations, protection from unlawful discrimination under the Equality Act 2010, and protection from detriment for whistleblowing or raising genuine health and safety concerns. After 12 weeks on the same assignment, agency workers on zero-hours arrangements may also gain rights to equal pay and conditions with directly recruited staff doing the same job.

Exclusivity clauses

A clause preventing a zero-hours worker from taking work with another employer — an "exclusivity clause" — is unenforceable by law, regardless of what the contract says. This exists precisely because it would be unfair to stop someone earning elsewhere on a contract that offers them no guaranteed income at all. If your contract contains one and your employer tries to enforce it, it has no legal effect.

Common problems in practice

Frequent problem areas include shifts being cancelled at very short notice with no compensation, workers being effectively "punished" with fewer future shifts after turning work down or raising a complaint (which can, depending on the reason, amount to unlawful detriment), and holiday pay being calculated incorrectly or simply not paid at all. If any of these sound familiar, it's worth having your specific situation reviewed — mislabelled or mishandled zero-hours arrangements are one of the more common sources of unpaid-wage and holiday-pay claims.
