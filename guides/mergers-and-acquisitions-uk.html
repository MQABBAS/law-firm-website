Mergers and Acquisitions: A Legal Overview for UK Businesses

8 min read Updated August 2026 United Kingdom

Whether you're selling the business you built, buying a competitor, or bringing two companies together, an M&A transaction involves a specific legal process with its own vocabulary. Here's a plain-English overview of how a typical UK deal unfolds.

On this page

  1. Share purchase vs asset purchase
  2. The typical deal process
  3. Due diligence
  4. The acquisition agreement
  5. Completion and beyond
  6. Frequently asked questions

Share purchase vs asset purchase

Most UK acquisitions of a private company take one of two structures:

  • Share purchase — the buyer acquires the target company itself by buying its shares. The company continues to exist as before, with all its assets, contracts, employees, and liabilities intact — including any hidden ones, which is why due diligence matters so much.
  • Asset purchase — the buyer acquires specific chosen assets (and sometimes liabilities) directly, rather than the company as a whole. This gives the buyer more control over exactly what they take on, but can be more complex to execute where contracts need to be individually transferred or novated.

The right structure depends on tax treatment, liability appetite, and the nature of the target business — this is usually one of the first strategic decisions in a deal.

The typical deal process

  1. Heads of terms — the key commercial terms are agreed in principle, usually non-binding except for confidentiality and exclusivity provisions.
  2. Due diligence — the buyer investigates the target business in detail.
  3. Negotiating the acquisition agreement — the detailed legal contract governing the sale.
  4. Signing and completion — sometimes simultaneous, sometimes separated by a gap while conditions are satisfied (e.g. regulatory approval).
  5. Post-completion integration — bringing the businesses, teams, and systems together.

Due diligence

Due diligence is the buyer's opportunity to look under the hood before committing. It typically covers financial records, material contracts, employment arrangements, intellectual property ownership, litigation and compliance history, and property. What's uncovered often directly affects price, or leads to specific warranties and indemnities being negotiated into the agreement to address identified risks.

Warranties vs indemnities — they're not the same

A warranty is a statement about the business that, if untrue, gives rise to a damages claim (subject to proving loss). An indemnity is a direct promise to reimburse the buyer pound-for-pound for a specific identified risk — a much stronger form of protection, typically reserved for known or high-risk issues uncovered in due diligence.

The acquisition agreement

The core legal document — a Share Purchase Agreement (SPA) or Asset Purchase Agreement (APA) — sets out the price and payment mechanism, warranties and indemnities, restrictive covenants (preventing the seller from competing or poaching staff afterwards), and conditions that must be satisfied before completion. This is usually the most heavily negotiated document in the whole transaction.

Completion and beyond

At completion, ownership formally transfers, funds move, and any Companies House filings are made. Many deals include a period of post-completion adjustment (e.g. based on final accounts) and an earn-out structure, where part of the price depends on the business hitting future targets — which requires its own careful drafting to avoid disputes later.

This guide describes general principles of UK M&A transactions as at the date above and is for general information only — it is not legal or tax advice. Every deal is different, so we'd recommend a consultation early in the process, ideally before heads of terms are signed.
