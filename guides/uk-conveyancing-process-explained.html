The UK Conveyancing Process Explained Step by Step

10 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales

Conveyancing is the legal process of transferring ownership of property from seller to buyer. It can feel opaque from the outside — long silences followed by sudden urgency — but it follows a fairly consistent sequence of stages. Here's what actually happens, in order, and roughly how long each part takes.

Instructing a conveyancer

Once an offer is accepted, both buyer and seller instruct a solicitor or licensed conveyancer. The seller's solicitor prepares a draft contract pack, including the title register, title plan, and a property information form completed by the seller (the "TA6"). The buyer's solicitor reviews this pack and raises initial enquiries about anything unclear — boundaries, disputes, alterations, guarantees for building work, and so on.

Searches and enquiries

The buyer's solicitor orders a set of searches against the property, typically including:

  • Local authority search — planning history, building control records, road and rail schemes affecting the area.
  • Water and drainage search — confirms how the property connects to the public network.
  • Environmental search — flags contamination risk, flood risk, and subsidence indicators.

Results often prompt follow-up questions to the seller's solicitor. This back-and-forth — "raising enquiries" — is usually the slowest part of the process, and delays here are the most common reason a transaction takes longer than expected.

Mortgage offer and survey

In parallel, if the buyer is borrowing, the lender arranges a mortgage valuation (a basic check the property is worth what's being paid — not a structural survey) and issues a formal mortgage offer once satisfied. Many buyers commission a more detailed independent survey (a HomeBuyer Report or full structural survey) to check the property's condition before committing — this is separate from, and more thorough than, the lender's valuation.

Leasehold adds a step

If the property is leasehold, the buyer's solicitor also needs to review the lease terms, obtain management pack information from the freeholder or managing agent, and check ground rent and service charge history — see our guide on leasehold vs freehold for more detail.

Exchange of contracts

Once all enquiries are resolved, the mortgage offer is in place, and everyone in the chain is ready, both sides sign identical contracts. At exchange, the buyer pays a deposit (commonly 10%, though this can be negotiated) and the transaction becomes legally binding — neither party can pull out without financial consequences from this point. A completion date is fixed at exchange.

Completion day

On completion day, the buyer's solicitor transfers the remaining funds to the seller's solicitor. Once received, the seller's solicitor confirms completion, keys are released (usually via the estate agent), and the buyer's solicitor registers the change of ownership with HM Land Registry and pays any Stamp Duty Land Tax due.

Typical timeline

For a straightforward, chain-free purchase, 8 to 12 weeks from offer to completion is a reasonable estimate. Realistically, most transactions involve at least one link in a chain, which adds coordination risk — everyone in the chain needs to be ready to exchange at the same time. Leasehold properties, listed buildings, or unusual search results can extend the timeline further.

This guide describes the general conveyancing process in England & Wales as at the date above and is for general information only — it is not legal advice. Scotland has a different system (missives rather than exchange/completion). Every transaction is different, so get in touch if you'd like a clearer picture of your specific purchase or sale.
