HMO Licensing for Landlords: What You Need to Know

7 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales

Renting out a property to sharers can be a good source of income, but Houses in Multiple Occupation are one of the most heavily regulated corners of the private rented sector — and getting licensing wrong can be an expensive mistake, both financially and in terms of your ability to recover the property later.

  3. Licence conditions and standards

What counts as an HMO?

Broadly, a property is a House in Multiple Occupation if it's occupied by at least three tenants, from more than one household, who share amenities like a kitchen, bathroom, or toilet. A "household" usually means a single person or people who are related or live together as a couple. Shared student housing, house-shares of young professionals, and some converted bedsit-style buildings are all common examples that can fall within the definition — but the exact rules and thresholds can be technical, so it's always worth checking a specific property's status rather than assuming.

Types of HMO licensing

There are three overlapping licensing regimes local authorities can operate:

  • Mandatory licensing — applies nationally to larger HMOs, broadly those occupied by five or more people forming more than one household, regardless of the number of storeys.
  • Additional licensing — a council can extend licensing requirements to smaller HMOs in its area (for example, three or four occupiers) if it decides this is needed to address problems such as poor management or overcrowding.
  • Selective licensing — some councils require a licence for any rented property in a designated area, HMO or not, often used to tackle issues like anti-social behaviour or low housing demand in a particular neighbourhood.
Always check with the local council directly

Licensing schemes vary significantly between local authorities and change over time. Before letting any shared property, check the specific council's current licensing requirements — don't assume a property is exempt just because it wasn't licensable in the past or isn't licensable in a different area.

Licence conditions and standards

An HMO licence typically comes with conditions covering fire safety (smoke alarms, fire doors, escape routes), minimum room sizes, adequate kitchen and bathroom facilities for the number of occupiers, waste disposal arrangements, and a requirement that the licence holder is a "fit and proper person" to manage the property — something the council will assess, including checking for relevant criminal convictions or previous enforcement action.

Penalties for non-compliance

Letting a property that requires a licence without one is a criminal offence, and the consequences can be severe: an unlimited fine on conviction or a civil penalty of up to £30,000 as an alternative to prosecution, a Rent Repayment Order which can require the landlord to repay up to 12 months' rent to the tenant(s) or the council, and — importantly — restrictions on serving a valid Section 21 "no fault" eviction notice while the property is unlicensed.

Applying for a licence

Applications are made to the relevant local authority, usually online, and typically require details of the property, the landlord and any managing agent, floor plans, and evidence of compliance with safety standards. Councils can and do inspect properties. Given how long applications can take to process, and that operating without a required licence is an offence from the moment it's needed, applying in good time — ideally before you start letting, or well before an existing licence expires — is essential.

This guide describes the general position on HMO licensing in England & Wales as at the date above and is for general information only — it is not legal advice. Licensing requirements vary by local authority and change over time, so get in touch to discuss your specific property.
