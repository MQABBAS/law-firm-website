Contesting a Will in the UK: Grounds and Process

8 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales

Not every will genuinely reflects what its maker intended, or was properly made in the first place. UK law allows a will to be challenged on specific grounds — and separately, allows certain people to claim they weren't left reasonably provided for, even from a perfectly valid will.

Challenging a will's validity

A will's validity can be challenged on several specific grounds:

  • Lack of testamentary capacity — the person making the will didn't have the mental capacity, at the time, to understand what they were doing.
  • Lack of valid execution — the formal signing and witnessing requirements weren't properly met (see our guide on how to write a will for what's required).
  • Lack of knowledge and approval — the person didn't actually know and approve the contents, which can arise where suspicious circumstances surround how the will was prepared.
  • Undue influence — the person was coerced or pressured into making a will they wouldn't otherwise have made.
  • Fraud or forgery — the will (or a signature on it) was fabricated.

Inheritance Act claims

A different type of claim — one that doesn't dispute the will's validity — is a claim under the Inheritance (Provision for Family and Dependants) Act 1975. This accepts the will is valid, but argues it (or the intestacy rules, if there's no will) failed to make reasonable financial provision for the claimant. Only people in specific eligible categories can bring this type of claim — including a spouse or civil partner, a former spouse who hasn't remarried, a child of the deceased, someone treated as a child of the family, or a person who was being financially maintained by the deceased.

These are two genuinely different types of claim

Challenging validity says "this isn't really their will." An Inheritance Act claim says "this is their will, but it didn't provide for me fairly." The right approach — and the evidence needed — differs significantly depending on which applies to your situation.

Time limits

There's no single fixed statutory deadline for challenging a will's validity, but delay can cause real practical problems — assets may already have been distributed and spent by the time a challenge is raised, making any eventual remedy harder to achieve. Inheritance Act claims, by contrast, carry a strict 6-month deadline from the date of the grant of probate (or letters of administration), though the court has limited discretion to extend this in some circumstances.

Stopping probate while you investigate

If you have concerns about a will but need time to investigate before deciding whether to formally challenge it, you can lodge a caveat at the Probate Registry. This prevents a grant of probate being issued while it's in place, effectively pausing the estate administration and giving breathing room to gather evidence and take advice before assets are distributed.

What evidence matters

Depending on the ground relied on, useful evidence can include medical records around the time the will was made (for capacity challenges), the solicitor's file if one was involved in drafting (often highly relevant to knowledge and approval and undue influence issues), witness accounts of the circumstances, and expert evidence on handwriting or document authenticity for fraud allegations.

This guide describes general principles of will and estate disputes in England & Wales as at the date above and is for general information only — it is not legal advice. These claims are time-sensitive and fact-specific, so get in touch promptly if you're considering a challenge.
