Commercial Lease Agreements: A Guide for UK Businesses

8 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales

A commercial lease is often one of the biggest and longest financial commitments a small business makes — and the terms are far more negotiable than most first-time tenants realise. Understanding the key clauses before signing can save serious money and hassle later.

Security of tenure

Under the Landlord and Tenant Act 1954, business tenants generally have a statutory right to remain in the property and request a new lease when the current term ends — "security of tenure." However, most commercial leases in practice are "contracted out" of this protection by agreement between landlord and tenant, following a specific statutory notice procedure before the lease is signed. Whether a lease is protected or contracted out fundamentally changes a tenant's position at the end of the term, so it's one of the first things to check.

Repairing obligations

Most commercial leases are structured as "full repairing and insuring" (FRI) leases, putting the cost of repair, maintenance, and insurance onto the tenant — even where the tenant occupies only part of a larger building, via a service charge covering shared areas and structure. This can represent a significant ongoing cost beyond the headline rent, and it's worth understanding the condition of the property (ideally via a survey) before agreeing to take on full repairing liability.

Watch for "full repairing" obligations on an old building

Signing an FRI lease on a building in poor condition can leave a tenant liable for extensive repair costs that have nothing to do with damage they caused. A schedule of condition, attached to the lease, can limit repairing obligations to the property's state at the start of the term rather than an unrealistic "as new" standard.

Rent reviews

Leases of any meaningful length usually include periodic rent reviews — commonly every 3 or 5 years — typically based on open market rent at the review date. Many are structured as "upward only", meaning rent can increase or stay the same at review, but never decrease, even if market rents have fallen. The specific assumptions and disregards used to calculate the reviewed rent are set out in detail in the lease and can significantly affect the outcome.

Break clauses

A break clause allows either party (or sometimes only the tenant, or only the landlord) to end the lease early on a specified date, subject to conditions — most commonly serving written notice within a defined window, and being up to date with rent and other lease obligations at the break date. Break clauses are notoriously easy to invalidate through a small procedural slip, so getting the mechanics right — and diarising key dates well in advance — really matters.

Other terms worth negotiating

Beyond the headline terms, it's worth paying attention to: permitted use clauses (what the property can actually be used for), alienation provisions (rules on assigning or subletting the lease), alterations clauses (what changes you can make to the space), and any personal guarantee or rent deposit required from the tenant, particularly for newer businesses without a long trading history.

This guide describes general principles of commercial leasing in England & Wales as at the date above and is for general information only — it is not legal advice. Commercial lease terms are heavily negotiable, so get advice before signing rather than after.
