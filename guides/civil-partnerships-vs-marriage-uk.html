Civil Partnerships vs Marriage: What's the Difference?

6 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales

Since opposite-sex civil partnerships became available in England and Wales, more couples than ever have a genuine choice between two distinct legal statuses. Understanding what actually differs — and what doesn't — can help you make the right choice for your relationship.

  2. Who can enter each
  3. Comparing the legal rights
  4. What actually differs

A brief background

Civil partnerships were originally introduced to give same-sex couples legal recognition before same-sex marriage became available. Once same-sex marriage was legalised, this raised an obvious question of fairness: why should opposite-sex couples not have access to the same alternative to marriage that same-sex couples enjoyed? The Civil Partnerships, Marriages and Deaths (Registration etc) Act 2019 resolved this by extending civil partnerships to opposite-sex couples in England and Wales.

Who can enter each

Today, both marriage and civil partnership are available to same-sex and opposite-sex couples alike. Neither can be entered into by someone already married or in a civil partnership, by close relatives, or by anyone under 18. The main practical difference at the point of formation is ceremonial: a marriage requires spoken words to solemnise it (a legally recognised form of words exchanged between the couple), while a civil partnership is formed by both parties signing a civil partnership document, without any requirement for spoken vows — some couples choose civil partnership specifically because they prefer this less ceremonial, more contractual character.

Both carry the same core legal protections

Whichever you choose, both marriage and civil partnership give you broadly the same legal standing when it comes to matters like next-of-kin rights, inheritance, and financial claims if the relationship later ends - a meaningful upgrade in legal protection compared with simply cohabiting, however long you've been together.

Comparing the legal rights

In almost every substantive respect, civil partners have the same rights as married couples: automatic inheritance rights on intestacy, the ability to make financial claims against each other on separation (following broadly the same principles as divorce), parental responsibility rules, pension rights, and next-of-kin status in medical and other contexts. For most couples, the practical legal consequences of choosing one over the other are minimal.

What actually differs

The remaining differences are mostly technical or ceremonial rather than substantive: the formation ceremony itself (spoken vows for marriage vs signing a document for civil partnership), the terminology used (spouse vs civil partner), and some historical nuances around adultery as a specific fact — adultery has no direct equivalent as grounds within civil partnership dissolution, though this has become less significant since the introduction of no-fault divorce and dissolution for both.

Ending a civil partnership or marriage

A marriage ends through divorce; a civil partnership ends through dissolution — but since the introduction of no-fault divorce, the two processes are now very closely aligned: both require a minimum notice period, an application, and a final order (the civil partnership equivalent of a decree absolute) that legally ends the relationship. Financial claims on separation follow essentially the same legal framework in both cases.
