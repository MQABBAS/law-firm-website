Grandparents' Rights and Contact in the UK

6 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales

When a family relationship breaks down — whether through parental separation or a wider family rift — grandparents can find themselves cut off from grandchildren with surprisingly little legal standing to do anything about it. Here's the actual legal position, and what options exist.

No automatic right to contact

Unlike parents, grandparents have no automatic legal right to contact with their grandchildren in England and Wales — no matter how significant the relationship has historically been. This surprises many grandparents, who often assume some baseline entitlement exists simply by virtue of the relationship. It doesn't; any right to contact has to be actively established through the family court.

Applying for a Child Arrangements Order

Grandparents can apply to the family court for a Child Arrangements Order to formalise contact — the same type of order used for disputes between separated parents. This can specify contact arrangements, including in-person visits, phone or video calls, and holiday periods, depending on what's appropriate for the specific family situation.

Getting the court's permission first

Unlike a parent, a grandparent generally needs the court's permission (called "leave") before they can even make the application. In deciding whether to grant leave, the court considers factors including the nature of the grandparent's proposed application, their connection with the child, and whether the application might disrupt the child's life to an extent that it would be harmful. In practice, leave is often granted where a grandparent has had a genuine, established relationship with the child.

A step most people don't expect

Needing court permission just to apply — before the actual merits of contact are even considered — is one of the most surprising and frustrating aspects of the process for grandparents encountering it for the first time.

How the court decides

If leave is granted, the case proceeds like any other Child Arrangements Order application: the child's welfare is the court's paramount consideration, assessed under the Children Act 1989 welfare checklist — including the child's own wishes and feelings (depending on age and maturity), their needs, and the likely effect of maintaining or losing the relationship with the grandparent.

Before going to court

As with most family disputes, grandparents are generally expected to attempt mediation and attend a Mediation Information and Assessment Meeting (MIAM) before applying to court, unless an exemption applies (such as safeguarding concerns). Court proceedings, even where ultimately successful, can take time and add strain to an already difficult family situation, so exploring a resolution directly — sometimes with the help of a solicitor's letter — is often worth trying first.
