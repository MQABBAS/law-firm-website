Shareholders' Agreements: Why Every UK Company Needs One

7 min read Updated August 2026 United Kingdom

Most co-founders start a company on good terms and don't want to think about what happens if things go wrong. That's exactly why a shareholders' agreement matters — it's the document that protects everyone precisely when relations are at their most strained, not when they're fine.

Why articles of association aren't enough

Every UK company has articles of association, and most small companies adopt Companies House's default "model articles." These are a reasonable baseline, but they're a public document designed for the general case — they don't address the specific commercial deal between your particular shareholders, and they can be changed by majority vote, which offers little protection to a minority shareholder.

What a shareholders' agreement covers

A well-drafted shareholders' agreement is a private contract that typically covers:

  • Decision-making — which decisions need unanimous consent versus a simple majority (e.g. taking on debt, issuing new shares, changing the business).
  • Share transfer restrictions — pre-emption rights, so existing shareholders get first refusal before shares are sold to an outsider.
  • Drag-along and tag-along rights — protecting both majority and minority shareholders on an exit or sale.
  • Founder vesting — shares earned over time, so a founder who leaves early doesn't keep a full stake.
  • Dividend policy and reinvestment expectations.
  • Non-compete and confidentiality obligations on shareholders.

What happens if someone wants to leave

One of the most valuable functions of a shareholders' agreement is setting out, in advance, what happens when a shareholder wants — or needs — to leave: resignation, retirement, dismissal as a director, incapacity, or death. Without provisions for this, resolving it can become expensive and adversarial, sometimes ending up in court.

"Good leaver / bad leaver" provisions

Many agreements distinguish between a "good leaver" (e.g. someone leaving due to ill health or after a set vesting period) and a "bad leaver" (e.g. dismissed for misconduct or resigning to join a competitor), with different share buy-back terms for each. This is one of the most commonly negotiated — and most important — parts of the agreement.

Deadlock provisions

Where two shareholders hold equal stakes (a common structure for two co-founders), a genuine disagreement can bring the company to a standstill with no way to break the tie. Deadlock provisions set out a resolution process in advance — escalation to an independent mediator, a casting vote mechanism, or a "shotgun" buy-sell clause where one party offers to buy or sell at a price, and the other must choose which.

When to put one in place

Ideally, before or immediately after incorporation, while relations are good and everyone can negotiate calmly and objectively. It's considerably harder — sometimes impossible — to agree these terms once a dispute has already started. If your company doesn't have one yet and has more than one shareholder, it's worth addressing sooner rather than later.
