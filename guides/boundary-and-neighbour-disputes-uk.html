Boundary and Neighbour Disputes: A UK Legal Guide

7 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales

Few legal disputes are as personally draining as a disagreement with a neighbour — precisely because you have to keep living next to each other afterwards. Boundary and neighbour disputes are also notorious for costing far more in legal fees than the disputed land is actually worth, which makes getting early advice on strategy especially important.

How boundaries are legally defined

Many people assume the Land Registry title plan shows an exact boundary line — it usually doesn't. Most titles show a "general boundary" only, which indicates the approximate position without determining the precise legal line. Establishing an exact boundary, where it's genuinely disputed, typically involves examining historical title deeds and conveyances, and — where that's not conclusive — potentially applying to the Land Registry for a "determined boundary," a more formal and definitive process.

The Party Wall Act

The Party Wall etc. Act 1996 applies to certain building work affecting a shared wall, structures on a boundary, or excavation near a neighbouring property. It requires the person doing the work to serve formal notice on affected neighbours before starting, giving them the chance to consent or dissent. If a neighbour dissents, the Act provides a structured process — typically involving a party wall surveyor — to resolve the disagreement without needing to go to court.

Skipping party wall notice can cause real problems

Building work covered by the Act without proper notice can lead to an injunction stopping the work, and potential liability for any damage caused — even where the work itself would otherwise have been perfectly lawful. It's a procedural step worth taking seriously.

Trees and overhanging branches

You're generally entitled to cut back branches or roots that cross onto your property, up to the boundary line — but not beyond it without permission, and any cuttings technically remain the tree owner's property (offering them back is the safe approach). Tree preservation orders and conservation area rules can restrict what work is allowed on certain trees, so it's worth checking before doing anything significant.

Resolving disputes without court

Given how costly boundary litigation can become relative to the value involved, sensible early steps include: a calm, direct conversation where possible, a solicitor's letter setting out the position clearly, obtaining an independent chartered surveyor's opinion on the boundary, and mediation — which courts increasingly expect parties to have attempted before resorting to litigation.

When court becomes necessary

Where a boundary genuinely can't be agreed and significant value or principle is at stake, court proceedings — or an application to the Land Registry's determined boundary process — may be unavoidable. Because costs can quickly outstrip the value of the disputed land, it's always worth getting a realistic view of costs versus outcome early, rather than being drawn into an expensive dispute on principle alone.

This guide describes general principles of English property law as at the date above and is for general information only — it is not legal advice. Boundary disputes turn heavily on the specific facts and documents involved, so get in touch to discuss your situation.
