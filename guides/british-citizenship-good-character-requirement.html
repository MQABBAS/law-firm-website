British Citizenship and the Good Character Requirement

7 min read Updated August 2026 UK-wide

Applying for British citizenship is often the final step of a long immigration journey — but the "good character" requirement catches out more applicants than many expect, sometimes for issues people didn't realise were relevant at all.

On this page

  1. The naturalisation route
  2. What "good character" means
  3. Criminal convictions
  4. Immigration history
  5. Financial and other conduct
  6. Frequently asked questions

The naturalisation route

Most adults become British citizens through naturalisation, which generally requires holding Indefinite Leave to Remain (or equivalent settled status) for a set period, meeting residence requirements, passing the Life in the UK test, meeting an English language requirement, and satisfying the good character requirement — a broad, discretionary assessment that applies to all applicants aged 10 and over.

What "good character" means

Good character is deliberately broad and not exhaustively defined. Home Office guidance considers factors including criminal history, immigration law compliance, financial soundness (including tax affairs and unpaid debts to public bodies), any involvement in war crimes, terrorism, or other notorious behaviour, and — more generally — a holistic view of whether the applicant is considered a suitable person to become a British citizen. It is assessed at the discretion of the decision-maker, guided by published policy.

Disclose everything relevant, even old or seemingly minor matters

Failing to disclose a relevant conviction, caution, or immigration issue - even an old one you assumed wouldn't matter - can itself be treated as dishonesty and cause far more serious problems than the underlying issue would have on its own. When in doubt, disclose and explain.

Criminal convictions

Published guidance sets out specific time periods, based on the length of any custodial sentence, during which a conviction will normally lead to refusal — for example, longer custodial sentences carry much longer (sometimes permanent) periods during which an application is expected to be refused, while shorter sentences and non-custodial disposals carry shorter relevant periods. Even convictions falling outside these specific windows can still be taken into account as part of the wider character assessment.

Immigration history

A history of overstaying a previous visa, working in breach of conditions, or — most seriously — providing false or misleading information in a previous application, can weigh heavily against a good character finding, sometimes for a considerable period afterwards, even where the applicant has since regularised their status and now holds ILR entirely lawfully.

Financial and other conduct

The assessment also considers matters such as significant unpaid tax, undischarged bankruptcy, and outstanding debts to public bodies like the NHS. While a single minor financial issue is unlikely to be determinative on its own, a pattern of financial non-compliance, or a substantial and unresolved debt, can be a genuine factor in a refusal.

This guide describes the general position on British citizenship applications as at the date above and is for general information only — it is not immigration advice. If you're unsure how something in your history might affect a citizenship application, get advice before applying rather than after a refusal.
