Data Subject Access Requests (DSARs): A Practical Guide

7 min read Updated August 2026 UK-wide

Whether you want to know what your employer has written about you, what a former partner's solicitor has recorded, or simply what a company holds on file, UK data protection law gives you a powerful and often underused right: the right to ask, and to be told.

On this page

  1. What is a DSAR?
  2. How to make a request
  3. What the organisation must provide

What is a DSAR?

A Data Subject Access Request is a request made under the UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018, asking an organisation to confirm whether it holds personal data about you and, if so, to provide a copy of it along with certain supporting information — such as why it's being processed, who it's been shared with, and how long it will be kept. Almost any organisation that holds personal data about you — an employer, a bank, a former landlord, a hospital, a social media company — can be the subject of a DSAR.

How to make a request

There's no required format — a DSAR can be made verbally or in writing, including by email or through social media, and doesn't need to mention "GDPR" or "Data Subject Access Request" explicitly, so long as it's reasonably clear the person is asking for their personal data. That said, putting a request in writing, clearly labelled as a DSAR, with as much detail as possible about what you're looking for, makes it far easier for the organisation to process quickly and correctly, and gives you clear evidence of when it was made.

Be as specific as you reasonably can

While you're entitled to ask for "all" personal data held about you, being specific about the department, date range, or type of information you're interested in (for example, "all emails mentioning me from HR between January and June") can significantly speed up the response and reduce the risk of an organisation claiming the request is too broad or burdensome.

What the organisation must provide

The organisation generally has one calendar month from receipt to respond, though this can be extended by up to two further months for complex or numerous requests, provided it tells you within the first month that it's doing so and explains why. The response should include a copy of your personal data, in an accessible format, plus information about the purposes of processing, categories of data, who it's been or will be shared with, how long it will be kept, and your other data protection rights (such as rectification or erasure).

Exemptions and limits

The right of access isn't unlimited. Certain categories of information can be withheld or redacted — for example, information that would identify another individual (unless that person consents, or it's reasonable to disclose without consent), certain legally privileged material, and some information connected to ongoing negotiations, crime prevention, or national security. Organisations can also refuse or charge a reasonable fee for requests that are genuinely "manifestly unfounded or excessive," though this is a high bar and is often misused as an excuse to avoid complying properly.

When things go wrong

If a DSAR is ignored, unreasonably delayed, or the response is clearly incomplete, the first step is usually to raise this directly with the organisation, referencing the original request date. If that doesn't resolve things, you can complain to the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO), the UK's data protection regulator, which can investigate and take enforcement action. Where non-compliance has caused you real damage or distress — for example, in the context of a wider dispute where withheld information matters — it may also be possible to bring a claim in the courts.

This guide describes the general position on Data Subject Access Requests in the UK as at the date above and is for general information only — it is not legal advice. If an organisation isn't responding properly to your DSAR, get in touch to discuss your options.
