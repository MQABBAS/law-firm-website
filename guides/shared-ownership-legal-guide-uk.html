Shared Ownership and Help to Buy: Legal Considerations in the UK

7 min read Updated August 2026 England

Shared ownership schemes help buyers who can't afford a full mortgage get a foot on the property ladder. They're a genuinely useful route into homeownership for many people — but the legal structure is more complex than a standard purchase, and it's worth understanding exactly what you're signing up for.

  2. Staircasing
  3. What you're responsible for

How shared ownership works

Under shared ownership, you buy a share of a property — commonly between 10% and 75% — funded through a mortgage and deposit on that share, while paying subsidised rent to a housing association on the remaining share, which it continues to own. You hold the property under a specific long lease designed for shared ownership, rather than owning it outright.

Staircasing

Staircasing is the process of buying additional shares over time — as your finances allow — which reduces the rent you pay correspondingly on the smaller remaining share. Depending on the specific scheme and lease, it may be possible to staircase all the way to full (100%) ownership, at which point the lease may convert or you become the outright owner, freeing you from the shared ownership structure entirely.

What you're responsible for

A point that surprises many buyers: even though you might only own, say, 40% of the property, most shared ownership leases make you responsible for repairs and maintenance of the whole property, not just your share — alongside service charges for any communal areas. This is a meaningful ongoing cost that should be factored into affordability from the outset, not treated as an afterthought.

Read the lease before you commit

Shared ownership leases vary between housing providers and can include different rules on staircasing, subletting, and rent review — don't assume all shared ownership schemes work identically. A solicitor reviewing the specific lease before exchange is genuinely valuable here.

Selling a shared ownership home

Selling is more restricted than a standard sale. Many leases give the housing association a nomination period — a set window in which they can try to find their own buyer for your share (often from their waiting list) — before you're free to market the property on the open market yourself. This can slow down a sale compared to a conventional property, which is worth factoring in if you expect to need to move within a few years.

Practical risks to understand

Shared ownership can work very well, but it's worth going in with clear eyes about the trade-offs: combined mortgage and rent costs aren't always dramatically cheaper than a conventional mortgage on a comparable full-price property once rent increases are factored in over time; full repair liability regardless of share owned; and a potentially smaller pool of buyers when it's time to sell, given the restrictions involved.

This guide describes the general structure of shared ownership schemes in England as at the date above and is for general information only — it is not legal or financial advice. Scheme rules vary between housing providers, so get in touch for advice on a specific property or lease.
