Debt Recovery for UK Businesses: Your Legal Options

7 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales

Unpaid invoices are one of the most common — and most stressful — problems small businesses face. The good news is there's a clear, structured legal process for chasing debts, with several escalation points before you ever need to go anywhere near a courtroom.

Early-stage recovery

Before anything formal, most businesses start with reminder emails or calls, and it's worth checking your original contract or terms of business for what it says about late payment, including any interest clause. Many disputes resolve at this stage — a debtor who's simply disorganised responds differently to one who's genuinely unable or unwilling to pay, and it's worth working out early which you're dealing with.

The letter before action

Before issuing court proceedings, you're expected to send a formal letter before action (sometimes called a letter before claim), setting out the debt, how it arose, and a reasonable deadline to pay before you escalate. This isn't just a courtesy — courts expect parties to follow the relevant pre-action protocol, and can penalise a claimant in costs later for skipping this step unreasonably, even if they ultimately win.

Statutory demands

For debts owed by a company above a set minimum threshold, a statutory demand is a powerful tool: it's a formal demand giving the debtor 21 days to pay or dispute the debt. If they do neither, the creditor can use the unpaid statutory demand as grounds to petition to wind up the company. The prospect of a winding-up petition — which can seriously damage a company's ability to trade, access finance, or maintain supplier relationships — often prompts payment very quickly.

Don't use a statutory demand for a genuinely disputed debt

Statutory demands are for debts that are due and not genuinely disputed. If the debtor has a real, arguable dispute about the amount owed, using this route can backfire — the demand can be set aside by the court, and you may face a costs order against you.

Court proceedings

If informal steps fail, a claim can be issued through the County Court, often via Money Claim Online for straightforward debts. Lower-value, uncomplicated claims are typically allocated to the small claims track — see our dedicated guide on small claims court for how that process works. If the debtor doesn't respond or defend, you can usually obtain a default judgment relatively quickly; a defended claim takes longer and follows a more structured court timetable.

Enforcing a judgment

Winning a judgment doesn't automatically mean you get paid — if the debtor still doesn't pay voluntarily, enforcement action may be needed, such as instructing bailiffs (enforcement agents), obtaining a charging order against property, or a third-party debt order against money the debtor is owed by someone else (such as their bank). Which method makes sense depends heavily on what assets the debtor actually has.

This guide describes the general debt recovery process in England & Wales as at the date above and is for general information only — it is not legal advice. Recovery strategy should be tailored to the specific debt and debtor, so get in touch to discuss the best approach for your situation.
