No Win No Fee Claims Explained: How They Work in the UK

6 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales

"No win, no fee" is a familiar phrase from advertising, but the legal mechanics behind it are worth understanding properly before you sign anything — particularly what happens to your compensation if the claim succeeds, and what (if anything) you're still on the hook for if it doesn't.

What a Conditional Fee Agreement actually is

A Conditional Fee Agreement (CFA) — the formal name for "no win no fee" — is an arrangement where your solicitor's basic fee only becomes payable if your claim succeeds. If the claim is unsuccessful, you typically don't pay your own solicitor's basic fee for the work carried out. This shifts a significant portion of the financial risk onto the solicitor, which is precisely why solicitors will generally only take on a CFA where they assess a claim as having a reasonable chance of success.

Success fees

If the claim succeeds, the solicitor is entitled to charge a success fee in addition to their normal fee, reflecting the risk they took on by not being paid if the case had failed. For personal injury claims specifically, success fees are capped by law at 25% of certain damages recovered (excluding damages for future care and losses), which limits how much of a claimant's compensation can be taken by the success fee.

ATE insurance

After-the-event (ATE) insurance is typically taken out once a claim has already arisen, to cover the risk of having to pay the other side's legal costs if the claim is lost — a separate risk from your own solicitor's fees. Many claimants take out ATE insurance alongside a CFA, and the premium is often deferred and only payable (usually from the compensation recovered) if the claim succeeds, meaning there's typically nothing to pay upfront either way.

"No win no fee" usually means genuinely low financial risk — not zero

Combined with ATE insurance, most personal injury claimants face very limited financial exposure if a claim doesn't succeed. But "usually" isn't "always" — it's worth reading the specific terms of your agreement rather than assuming every scenario is covered.

What "no win no fee" doesn't always cover

It's worth checking the agreement for circumstances that could still leave you liable for some costs — for example, withdrawing the claim yourself against advice, being found to have exaggerated or misrepresented the claim, or failing to cooperate reasonably with your solicitor (missing appointments, not providing evidence, and so on). These aren't the norm, but they exist in most standard CFA terms.

Questions worth asking before signing

Before agreeing to a CFA, it's reasonable to ask: exactly what percentage is the success fee, and is ATE insurance included or arranged separately; what specific circumstances (if any) could make me liable for costs even if I lose; and how are disbursements (expenses like medical reports or court fees) handled along the way.

This guide describes the general framework for Conditional Fee Agreements in England & Wales as at the date above and is for general information only — it is not legal advice. Terms vary between firms and cases, so always read your specific agreement carefully and ask questions before signing.
