Landlord and Tenant Disputes: Legal Rights in the UK

8 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales

The relationship between landlords and tenants is governed by a dense web of legislation designed to balance a landlord's right to their property against a tenant's right to a safe, stable home. Most disputes fall into a handful of recurring categories — here's what the law says about each.

Eviction: Section 21 and Section 8

For an assured shorthold tenancy (the standard type of residential tenancy), a landlord seeking possession generally uses one of two routes:

  • Section 21 — often called a "no-fault" eviction, this lets a landlord seek possession without stating a specific reason, but only if strict procedural requirements have been met (deposit protection, licensing, prescribed information, and more).
  • Section 8 — used where the landlord relies on a specific statutory ground, most commonly rent arrears, and requires proving that ground to the court.

This area has been the subject of ongoing reform debate, including proposals to remove Section 21 entirely — so both landlords and tenants should check the current legal position rather than relying on older assumptions.

Deposit protection

Landlords taking a deposit for an assured shorthold tenancy must protect it in a government-approved tenancy deposit scheme within 30 days, and give the tenant prescribed information about where it's held. Failing to comply can prevent the landlord from serving a valid Section 21 notice and can lead to a court ordering compensation to the tenant of up to three times the deposit amount.

Repairs and disrepair

Under section 11 of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, landlords are generally responsible for keeping the structure and exterior of the property in repair, and for the installations providing water, gas, electricity, heating, and sanitation. Tenants are expected to use the property properly and report problems promptly. Where a landlord fails to carry out repairs they're responsible for after being notified, a tenant may have a claim for disrepair, potentially including compensation.

Document everything

In both repairs disputes and deposit disputes, a clear paper trail — photos, dated messages, and records of when issues were reported — makes an enormous practical difference if a dispute ends up before a court or an adjudication scheme.

Rent arrears and rent increases

Rent arrears are one of the most common grounds for a Section 8 notice, with different mandatory and discretionary grounds depending on how much is owed and for how long. Rent increases during a tenancy generally require following a specific statutory process (or the process set out in the tenancy agreement) — a landlord can't simply demand more rent without following the correct procedure, and a tenant can challenge an increase they consider excessive through the relevant tribunal.

Unlawful eviction and harassment

It's a criminal offence for a landlord to evict a tenant without a valid court order — including changing locks, removing belongings, or cutting off utilities to force someone out. This applies even where the landlord has valid grounds for possession; the correct legal process still has to be followed through to a court order and, if needed, bailiff enforcement.
