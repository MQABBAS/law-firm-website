Road Traffic Accident Claims: A UK Legal Guide

7 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales

Road traffic accidents are the most common source of personal injury claims in the UK, and the process has changed significantly in recent years — particularly for whiplash injuries, which now follow a specific reformed process. Here's what to expect.

Immediately after an accident

Where safe to do so: exchange details with other drivers involved (name, contact details, registration, and insurance details), take photographs of the scene, vehicle positions and damage, and any visible injuries, note down contact details for independent witnesses, and report the accident to the police where required (for example, if anyone is injured or details can't be exchanged at the scene). Seek medical attention promptly, even for injuries that seem minor at first — this both protects your health and creates a medical record linking the injury to the accident.

Whiplash reforms and the Official Injury Claim service

Since 2021, most lower-value whiplash claims arising from road traffic accidents in England and Wales go through the Official Injury Claim online service rather than the traditional claims process. This system applies a fixed compensation tariff based on the severity and duration of the whiplash injury, rather than the case-by-case general damages assessment used for other types of personal injury claim. This reform specifically targets whiplash and doesn't apply to more serious injuries, which continue to follow the standard claims process.

Not all RTA injuries fall under the tariff system

More serious injuries arising from a road traffic accident — beyond straightforward whiplash — are generally still assessed under the traditional approach, which can result in significantly different compensation levels. It's worth getting advice on which process actually applies to your specific injuries.

How fault is determined

Fault is usually established through a combination of witness accounts, the police report (where one exists), vehicle damage patterns, dashcam or CCTV footage, and each driver's account of events. In some cases, fault is shared between both drivers ("contributory negligence"), which can reduce — but not necessarily eliminate — the compensation available to a claimant found partly responsible.

Uninsured and untraced drivers

If the at-fault driver has no valid insurance, or can't be traced (a hit-and-run, for example), a claim can often still be pursued through the Motor Insurers' Bureau (MIB) — a scheme funded by a levy on the insurance industry specifically to compensate victims in these situations, so the absence of insurance on the other side doesn't necessarily mean no recovery is possible.

What affects the value of a claim

Beyond the injury itself, a claim's value can reflect ongoing medical or rehabilitation costs, lost earnings (both past and, where relevant, future), damage to the vehicle and other property, and any care or assistance needed during recovery. Serious or long-term injuries generally require more detailed medical evidence to properly value the full extent of the claim.
