Settlement Agreements: What UK Employees Should Know

7 min read Updated August 2026 United Kingdom

Being offered a settlement agreement — sometimes still called a "compromise agreement" — can feel sudden and unsettling. But the legal process around them exists specifically to protect employees, including a requirement that you get independent advice before you can be bound by one.

On this page

  1. What a settlement agreement does
  2. Why independent advice is required
  3. What's typically included
  4. What can be negotiated
  5. Tax treatment
  6. Frequently asked questions

What a settlement agreement does

A settlement agreement is a legally binding contract, usually signed when employment is ending, under which the employee agrees to waive their right to bring specified employment claims (such as unfair dismissal or discrimination) in exchange for a payment and, often, other terms — such as an agreed reference or confirmation of the reason for leaving. Once validly signed, it generally prevents the employee bringing those specific claims to an employment tribunal later.

Why independent advice is required

For a settlement agreement to be legally valid, the employee must receive advice from an independent, qualified adviser — normally a solicitor — on the terms of the agreement and its effect, particularly on the employee's ability to bring a tribunal claim. This isn't optional box-ticking: without it, the agreement simply isn't binding, regardless of what either party intended.

Your employer usually pays for this advice

Because the independent advice requirement exists for the employer's benefit too (it's what makes the waiver enforceable), it's standard practice for the employer to contribute towards the employee's legal costs — commonly a fixed contribution set out in the agreement itself.

What's typically included

Beyond the settlement payment, agreements commonly cover: confidentiality obligations, a mutually agreed reference or announcement of the departure, treatment of any outstanding notice period (worked or paid in lieu), continuation of benefits for a defined period, and sometimes restrictive covenants confirming or varying existing post-termination restrictions.

What can be negotiated

An initial offer isn't necessarily the final word. Depending on the strength of any underlying claims and the circumstances of the departure, there's often room to negotiate the settlement sum, the reference wording, the timing of payment, and specific terms like garden leave arrangements or extended benefits — this is precisely the kind of assessment an independent adviser is well placed to help with.

Tax treatment

Tax treatment isn't uniform across the whole payment. A genuine ex gratia termination payment can often benefit from a tax-free allowance up to a set threshold, but other elements — such as payment in lieu of notice, or anything that's effectively deferred salary or bonus — are generally taxed as normal income. How the payment is structured and labelled in the agreement genuinely affects the tax outcome, which is another good reason to have it properly reviewed rather than just skim-read.

This guide describes general principles of settlement agreements in the UK as at the date above and is for general information only — it is not legal or tax advice. If you've been offered one, get in touch before signing anything — the independent advice requirement exists precisely so you get a proper review.
