Domestic Abuse: Non-Molestation and Occupation Orders in the UK

7 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales
If you're in immediate danger, call 999.

For confidential, free support at any time, the National Domestic Abuse Helpline is available 24/7 on 0808 2000 247. This guide explains the legal protection available, but please reach out for support first if you're at risk right now.

UK family courts have specific powers to protect people experiencing domestic abuse — legally binding orders that can require an abuser to stay away, or to leave the family home. Understanding what's available can make it easier to take the first step.

Non-molestation orders

A non-molestation order is a civil court order that prohibits someone from using or threatening violence against you, or from harassing, intimidating, or pestering you. It can also protect a relevant child. It's designed to be broad enough to capture a wide range of abusive behaviour, not just physical violence — including harassment, intimidation, and other forms of controlling or coercive conduct.

Breaching a non-molestation order is a criminal offence, punishable by arrest and prosecution — this gives it real teeth beyond a civil order that simply exists on paper.

Occupation orders

An occupation order regulates who can live in the family home. Depending on the circumstances, it can require an abusive partner to leave the home — even if they legally own or rent it — and can exclude them from entering a defined area around the property. The court weighs factors including the housing needs and resources of each party, the effect on any children, and the conduct of both parties when deciding whether, and on what terms, to grant one.

Who can apply

Protection is available to "associated persons" — a deliberately wide category under the Family Law Act 1996 that includes spouses and former spouses, civil partners, cohabitants and former cohabitants, people in an intimate personal relationship of significant duration, and other relatives. You don't need to be married, and you don't need to still be living with the person, to apply.

Emergency applications

Where there's a risk of significant harm, an application can be made "without notice" (also called ex parte) — meaning the court can grant an order before the other person is told about the application, to avoid tipping them off and increasing risk. A further hearing, with both parties present, is then usually listed shortly afterwards to review the order.

Legal aid may be available

Unlike many areas of family law, legal aid remains available for domestic abuse injunction applications for those who qualify financially, without needing to meet the same evidence requirements as for other family legal aid matters. See our guide on legal aid in the UK for more on eligibility.

What happens if an order is breached

Because breaching a non-molestation order is a criminal offence, the police can arrest someone immediately for breaching one, and it can lead to prosecution and a criminal record, separate from any family court proceedings. Occupation orders can also carry a power of arrest attached, depending on the terms set by the court.
