How to Get Divorced in the UK: Step-by-Step Guide

9 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales

Since April 2022, England and Wales have operated a "no-fault" divorce system, which removed the need to blame your spouse or prove a period of separation. This guide walks through the modern process from start to finish: who can apply, what it costs, how long it takes, and how it fits together with sorting out finances and arrangements for children.

Who can apply for a divorce

To apply for a divorce in England or Wales, you generally need to have been married for at least one year and to satisfy the court's jurisdiction rules — typically that you or your spouse are domiciled or habitually resident in England or Wales. Civil partners follow an equivalent "dissolution" process rather than divorce, but the practical steps are very similar.

Under the no-fault system, the only legal ground for divorce is that the marriage has broken down irretrievably. You simply make a statement to that effect — there's no need to select a reason such as unreasonable behaviour or adultery, and in most cases the statement can't be contested by the other party.

The step-by-step process

The modern divorce process is entirely online and follows a fixed sequence of stages:

  1. Apply for a divorce. One spouse (a "sole application") or both together (a "joint application") submit the divorce application to the court, along with the marriage certificate and the court fee.
  2. Acknowledgement of service. If it's a sole application, your spouse has 14 days to confirm they've received the papers.
  3. Apply for a Conditional Order. After a mandatory 20-week reflection period from the start of proceedings, you can apply for a Conditional Order — the court's confirmation that you're entitled to divorce.
  4. Conditional Order is granted. A judge reviews the application on paper; there's no need to attend court for an undefended divorce.
  5. Apply for the Final Order. At least 6 weeks after the Conditional Order, you can apply for the Final Order, which legally ends the marriage.
Why the waiting periods exist

The 20-week and 6-week reflection periods were built into the Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020 specifically to give couples time to reflect and, where appropriate, to reach agreement on practical arrangements — including finances — before the marriage is formally ended.

How long it takes

Assuming there are no delays or disputes, the minimum timeframe from application to Final Order is around 7 to 8 months, driven almost entirely by the two statutory waiting periods above. In practice, cases often take longer — particularly where there's a linked financial settlement to negotiate, where paperwork needs correcting, or where court processing times run behind.

What it costs

There are two separate costs to budget for:

  • The court fee — a fixed fee set by HM Courts & Tribunals Service, payable when you submit the application. A fee remission may be available if you're on a low income or receiving certain benefits.
  • Legal fees — many people apply for the divorce itself without a solicitor, since the online process is designed to be self-service. Legal advice becomes more valuable — and often essential — once finances, property, or pensions need to be divided, or where there's any disagreement.

Finances and the Final Order

One of the most important — and most overlooked — points in the whole process is that getting divorced does not, by itself, settle finances. Even an amicable split can leave both parties financially exposed if claims for property, savings, pensions, or maintenance are never formally closed off.

Where couples reach an agreement, it's usually turned into a financial consent order and approved by the court, making it legally binding. Without one, either party can potentially bring a financial claim against the other years later — even after remarriage in some circumstances. It's generally advisable to resolve finances before applying for the Final Order, and many solicitors recommend waiting for a financial consent order to be approved first.

Arrangements for children

Divorce proceedings and arrangements for children are handled separately. The court doesn't automatically decide living or contact arrangements as part of the divorce — parents are expected to agree these between themselves where possible, with mediation as a common first step if they can't. If agreement isn't possible, either parent can apply to the family court for a Child Arrangements Order. See our dedicated guide on child arrangements after separation for more detail.

This guide describes the general divorce process in England & Wales as at the date above and is for general information only — it is not legal advice and doesn't account for your personal circumstances. Scotland and Northern Ireland have different procedures. If you're considering divorce, we'd always recommend a short consultation before you start.
