Employment Contracts: Key Terms Every UK Employee Should Understand

8 min read Updated August 2026 United Kingdom

Most people sign their employment contract without reading it closely, and only look again when something goes wrong. Understanding the key terms upfront — especially the ones that matter most when a relationship changes or ends — puts you in a much stronger position later.

  2. Employee, worker, or self-employed?

The written statement of particulars

UK employees are legally entitled to a written statement of particulars from day one of employment, setting out core terms including pay, working hours, holiday entitlement, notice periods, place of work, and sick pay arrangements. This doesn't necessarily have to be one single "contract" document — some details can be in a staff handbook or separate policy — but the key terms must be provided in writing from the outset.

Employee, worker, or self-employed?

UK law recognises different working statuses with different levels of protection: employees have the fullest rights, including protection from unfair dismissal (subject to qualifying service) and redundancy rights; "workers" — a category that includes many gig-economy and casual roles — get some rights such as minimum wage and paid holiday, but not others like unfair dismissal protection; and genuinely self-employed contractors have the fewest statutory employment protections. Crucially, what your contract calls you isn't decisive — tribunals look at the actual reality of how the relationship works in practice.

Restrictive covenants

Restrictive covenants (sometimes called non-compete or non-solicitation clauses) try to limit what you can do after leaving — working for a competitor, poaching clients, or recruiting former colleagues, for a defined period. Courts will only enforce a covenant to the extent it goes no further than reasonably necessary to protect a genuine business interest (like trade secrets or client relationships) — overly broad restrictions on time, geography, or scope are often unenforceable, though that's ultimately a matter for a court to decide, not something to assume unilaterally.

Don't assume a covenant is unenforceable without advice

While overly broad covenants often fail, some are drafted carefully enough to hold up — and employers do sometimes pursue breaches through the courts. If you're planning a move that might trigger a restrictive covenant, it's worth getting it reviewed rather than guessing.

Changing contract terms

An employer generally can't unilaterally change your contractual terms — pay, hours, location, or duties — without your agreement. Where an employer wants to make changes and the employee won't agree, options are usually limited to negotiation, or the employer terminating the existing contract and offering re-engagement on new terms, which itself carries legal risk (including a potential unfair dismissal claim) if not handled properly.

Other clauses worth reading closely

It's worth paying particular attention to: notice periods (which apply both ways), any probationary period terms, garden leave provisions, intellectual property assignment clauses (especially relevant in creative or technical roles), and any deduction from wages clauses — which are only lawful in specific, limited circumstances under UK law.
