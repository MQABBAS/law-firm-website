TUPE Transfers Explained: What Happens to Your Job

7 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales

Hearing that "the business is being sold" or "the contract is going to another provider" can be alarming if you're one of the employees affected. TUPE exists precisely to protect employees in these situations — but understanding exactly what it does and doesn't guarantee makes a real difference to how you handle the transition.

  1. What is TUPE?

What is TUPE?

TUPE — the Transfer of Undertakings (Protection of Employment) Regulations — is designed to protect employees when the business they work for changes hands, or when the service they work on moves to a different provider. Rather than employment simply ending and a new job being offered on the new employer's terms, TUPE automatically transfers the employee's existing contract to the new employer, preserving continuity of employment as though there had been no break at all.

When does TUPE apply?

TUPE covers two broad scenarios: a business transfer, where a business or a distinct part of one is sold or otherwise changes ownership as a going concern, and a service provision change, which covers common outsourcing scenarios — for example, when a company outsources its cleaning, catering, or IT support to a contractor, when that contract later moves to a different contractor, or when a previously outsourced service is brought back in-house.

Not every business sale involves TUPE

A sale of company shares generally does not trigger TUPE, because the employer (the company) doesn't change — only its ownership does. TUPE is primarily about the underlying business or activity changing hands, not who owns the shares in the employing company.

What rights transfer with you

On a TUPE transfer, employees' existing terms and conditions — pay, hours, holiday, length of service, and most contractual rights — transfer automatically to the new employer, and continuity of employment is preserved for purposes like calculating redundancy pay or unfair dismissal qualifying periods later. Certain occupational pension rights are treated differently and don't automatically transfer in the same way, though there are separate statutory protections covering minimum pension contributions in many cases.

Information and consultation

Both the outgoing and incoming employer have legal obligations to inform, and where measures are proposed, consult with, affected employees (usually through recognised trade unions or elected employee representatives) about the transfer — who is affected, why it's happening, and any measures either employer envisages taking as a result. Failing to properly inform and consult can lead to a tribunal awarding compensation of up to 13 weeks' pay per affected employee.

Dismissal and changes to terms

Dismissing an employee, or materially changing their terms, is automatically unfair (or void, for a change of terms) if the transfer itself is the sole or principal reason — employers can't use a TUPE transfer as a convenient moment to cut pay or force people out. There is a narrow exception where there is a genuine economic, technical or organisational reason entailing changes in the workforce (often referred to as an "ETO reason") — for example, a genuine redundancy situation arising from the transfer — but even then, normal fair dismissal and redundancy procedures still need to be followed.

This guide describes the general position on TUPE transfers in England & Wales as at the date above and is for general information only — it is not legal advice. Whether TUPE applies to a particular situation depends on its specific facts, so get in touch to discuss yours.
