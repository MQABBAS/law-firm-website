UK Immigration Basics: Visas and the Right to Work

8 min read Updated August 2026 United Kingdom

UK immigration law affects individuals wanting to live, work, or study here, and the UK employers who want to hire them. Since Brexit, the system treats applicants from every country under broadly the same points-based framework. Here's a general overview of how it works.

The points-based system

Since 2021, the UK operates a points-based immigration system that applies equally to EU and non-EU nationals — a significant change from the pre-Brexit position, where EU citizens had free movement rights. For work-based routes, points are typically awarded for factors including a confirmed job offer from an approved sponsor, meeting minimum salary thresholds, and the skill level of the role, with applicants needing to reach a required points total to qualify.

Common visa routes

The UK visa system includes numerous distinct routes for different purposes, including (among others): the Skilled Worker route for sponsored employment, the Student route, Family routes for joining a spouse, partner, or family member already settled or with status in the UK, the Global Talent route for recognised leaders in certain fields, and various visitor routes for short stays. Each route has its own specific eligibility requirements, application process, and permitted length of stay.

Right to work checks for employers

UK employers are legally required to carry out right to work checks on all employees before they start work — this applies to British citizens as well as foreign nationals, since the check itself is what protects the employer, not an assumption based on nationality. Employers who fail to carry out proper checks can face significant civil penalties, and in serious cases (knowingly employing someone without the right to work), criminal liability.

Checks must be done correctly, not just done

A right to work check only provides a legal defence (a "statutory excuse") if carried out in the correct manner — checking original documents or using the Home Office's online checking service properly, and keeping appropriate records. A check done carelessly may not actually protect the employer.

Sponsor licences

To sponsor a migrant worker under routes like Skilled Worker, a UK employer generally needs a sponsor licence from the Home Office. This isn't a one-off approval — licensed sponsors take on ongoing compliance duties, including monitoring sponsored employees and reporting certain changes to the Home Office, with the licence subject to audit and potential revocation if duties aren't properly met.

Settlement and citizenship

Many visa routes lead, after a qualifying period (commonly five years, though this varies by route), to an application for Indefinite Leave to Remain (settlement) — removing the time limit on someone's stay. Settlement, in turn, is generally a precondition for applying for British citizenship at a later stage, subject to further specific requirements.

This guide describes general principles of UK immigration law as at the date above and is for general information only — it is not legal advice. Immigration rules change frequently and are highly fact-specific, so please get in touch for advice on your particular situation.
