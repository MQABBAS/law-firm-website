Trusts Explained: A Plain-English Guide

8 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales

Trusts have a reputation for being something only the very wealthy need — but in reality, they're a flexible legal tool used by all sorts of families for very practical reasons, from protecting a young child's inheritance to providing for a relative with a disability.

What is a trust?

At its core, a trust is a legal relationship involving three roles: the settlor (the person who creates the trust and puts assets into it), the trustees (who become the legal owners of those assets and are responsible for managing them), and the beneficiaries (who are entitled to benefit from the assets, according to the trust's terms). This "split" between legal ownership and the right to benefit is what makes trusts so flexible — assets can be professionally managed and protected while still ultimately being for someone else's benefit.

Why people set up trusts

Trusts are used for a wide range of practical reasons: holding money or property for children until they reach an age the settlor considers sensible to inherit outright, providing for a family member with a disability without affecting their entitlement to means-tested benefits, protecting family assets from being lost in a later divorce or bankruptcy of a beneficiary, holding property for someone who lacks mental capacity to manage it themselves, and estate and inheritance tax planning as part of a wider strategy.

A trust is not a way to avoid your responsibilities

Trusts are a legitimate and long-established legal tool, but they come with real legal obligations for trustees and genuine loss of control for the settlor over assets once transferred in. They should be set up with proper advice, not as a quick fix, and are rarely appropriate purely as a way of putting assets beyond the reach of creditors.

Common types of trust

Several common structures come up repeatedly in UK estate planning:

  • Bare trust — the simplest form; the beneficiary has an absolute right to both the trust capital and income, and can demand it once they turn 18. Often used for straightforward gifts to children.
  • Discretionary trust — trustees have discretion over how and when to distribute income or capital among a class of beneficiaries, offering flexibility to respond to beneficiaries' changing circumstances over time.
  • Interest in possession trust — a beneficiary has an immediate right to trust income (for example, a surviving spouse), while the capital is preserved for others (often children) after that beneficiary's interest ends.
  • Trusts for vulnerable beneficiaries or disabled persons — specially designed trusts that can benefit from more favourable tax treatment, aimed at supporting a beneficiary without jeopardising means-tested benefits.

The role of trustees

Being a trustee is a genuine legal responsibility, not an honorary title. Trustees owe strict fiduciary duties — they must act honestly, in the best interests of the beneficiaries, avoid conflicts of interest, invest trust assets prudently (guided by the Trustee Act 2000), keep clear accounts, and administer the trust strictly according to its terms. Trustees can be held personally liable for losses caused by a breach of these duties, which is why many people choose to appoint a professional trustee, or at least take legal advice before accepting the role.

Trusts and tax

Trust taxation is a genuinely complex area, and different types of trust are taxed differently for income tax, capital gains tax, and inheritance tax purposes — including, for many trusts, a periodic inheritance tax charge every ten years and an exit charge when capital leaves the trust. Because the rules are detailed and change relatively often, specialist tax advice alongside legal advice is strongly recommended both when setting up a trust and while it's running.
