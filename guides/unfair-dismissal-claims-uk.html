Unfair Dismissal Claims in the UK: Your Rights Explained

9 min read Updated August 2026 United Kingdom

Losing a job unexpectedly is stressful enough without also being unsure whether it was lawful. This guide explains what "unfair dismissal" actually means in UK law, who can claim, the process and deadlines involved, and what compensation might be available.

  1. What counts as unfair dismissal
  2. Who is eligible to claim
  3. Potentially fair reasons for dismissal
  4. The claims process

What counts as unfair dismissal

Under the Employment Rights Act 1996, a dismissal is unfair unless the employer can show it was for a potentially fair reason and that they acted reasonably in treating that reason as sufficient to dismiss — including following a fair process. Even where the underlying reason is legitimate, a dismissal can still be found unfair if the employer skipped proper procedure, such as failing to investigate, hold a disciplinary hearing, or allow an appeal.

Who is eligible to claim

For an ordinary unfair dismissal claim, you generally need at least two years' continuous employment with the same employer. However, certain dismissals are treated as automatically unfair regardless of length of service, including dismissal related to:

  • Pregnancy or maternity leave
  • Whistleblowing (making a protected disclosure)
  • Asserting a statutory employment right
  • Trade union membership or activities
  • Requesting flexible working in certain circumstances

Potentially fair reasons for dismissal

UK law recognises five potentially fair reasons for dismissal: conduct, capability (performance or health), redundancy, illegality (continuing to employ someone would break the law), and some other substantial reason. Falling into one of these categories is only the first step — the employer still needs to show the decision and process were reasonable in the circumstances.

Process matters as much as the reason

A surprisingly large number of successful unfair dismissal claims turn on procedural failures — no formal warning, no chance to respond, no right of appeal — rather than the underlying reason for dismissal being wrong. Employers are expected to follow the ACAS Code of Practice on disciplinary and grievance procedures.

The claims process

  1. Notify ACAS. Before lodging a tribunal claim, you must contact ACAS for early conciliation — a compulsory step that also gives both sides a chance to settle without a hearing.
  2. Early conciliation. ACAS will attempt to facilitate a resolution over a set period. Many cases settle at this stage.
  3. Lodge a claim (ET1). If conciliation doesn't resolve things, you can submit a claim to the Employment Tribunal.
  4. Employer's response (ET3). The employer responds, typically disputing some or all of the claim.
  5. Hearing. If not settled beforehand, the case proceeds to a tribunal hearing, where both sides present evidence.

Time limits

The standard deadline to start ACAS early conciliation is 3 months less one day from the effective date of termination. This is a strict deadline — the tribunal only has limited discretion to extend it, so missing it can mean losing the right to claim entirely, even with a strong case.

What compensation looks like

If a tribunal finds a dismissal unfair, compensation is usually made up of:

  • A basic award — calculated using a formula based on age, length of service, and weekly pay (capped), similar to statutory redundancy pay.
  • A compensatory award — reflecting your actual financial loss, such as lost earnings and benefits, subject to a statutory cap that's reviewed periodically.

In some cases, a tribunal can order reinstatement or re-engagement instead of, or alongside, compensation, though this is relatively uncommon in practice.

This guide describes the general unfair dismissal framework in Great Britain as at the date above and is for general information only — it is not legal advice. Time limits are strict, so if you think you may have a claim, we'd recommend getting advice as soon as possible rather than waiting.
