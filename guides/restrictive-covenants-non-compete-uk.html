Restrictive Covenants and Non-Compete Clauses in the UK

7 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales

Almost every senior employment contract and business sale agreement contains a clause trying to stop someone competing, poaching clients, or hiring away staff after they leave. Many people assume these clauses are automatically binding just because they signed — but UK law takes a much more skeptical view than that.

The basic legal principle

English law starts from the position that clauses restricting someone's ability to work or trade freely are, in principle, an unlawful restraint of trade and therefore void. A restrictive covenant is only enforceable if the party relying on it can show it protects a genuine legitimate business interest — such as trade secrets, client connections, or the stability of the workforce — and goes no further than reasonably necessary to protect that interest. Get either element wrong, and a court can strike the clause out entirely, leaving the other party free to act as though it never existed.

Common types of covenant

  • Non-compete — prevents working for, or setting up, a competing business for a defined period.
  • Non-solicitation — prevents approaching the former employer's or business's clients, customers, or staff to try to win their business or recruit them.
  • Non-dealing — goes further than non-solicitation, preventing doing business with former clients even where they approach you first.
  • Confidentiality — protects trade secrets and confidential information, and is generally easier to enforce than the others since it doesn't restrict someone's ability to work, only what they can do with specific information.
Narrower, shorter covenants are far more likely to be enforced

Courts are consistently more willing to enforce covenants that are precisely targeted — short in duration, limited to a specific geographic area or client list, and proportionate to the person's actual seniority and influence — than broad, catch-all clauses that try to restrict as much as possible "just in case."

What makes a covenant "reasonable"

Courts weigh factors including the duration of the restriction, its geographic scope, how precisely it's drafted, and — critically — the seniority and actual role of the person bound by it. A senior salesperson with deep client relationships is far more likely to be validly restricted from poaching clients than a junior employee with little client contact, even under an identically worded clause, because the "legitimate interest" being protected differs so much between them.

Covenants in employment vs business sales

Courts generally scrutinise covenants in employment contracts more strictly than those agreed as part of a business sale — for example, where a seller of a business agrees not to compete with the buyer afterwards. This is because a business sale is typically negotiated between parties of more equal bargaining power, often with the seller receiving significant payment specifically in exchange for agreeing not to compete, whereas an employee has comparatively little bargaining power when accepting a job offer.

Enforcing or challenging a covenant

An employer or business wanting to enforce a covenant will typically need to act quickly, often seeking an urgent injunction to stop the breach before real damage is done — waiting too long can itself undermine the case. Someone wanting to challenge a covenant they're bound by should have it reviewed carefully against the reasonableness principles above before simply assuming it's binding, or alternatively before simply ignoring it and risking litigation.

This guide describes the general position on restrictive covenants in England & Wales as at the date above and is for general information only — it is not legal advice. Whether a specific covenant is enforceable depends heavily on its precise wording and context, so get in touch to have yours reviewed.
