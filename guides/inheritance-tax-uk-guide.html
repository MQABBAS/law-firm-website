Inheritance Tax in the UK: What You Need to Know

8 min read Updated August 2026 United Kingdom

Inheritance Tax has a reputation for catching families by surprise — partly because property values have risen faster than the tax-free thresholds, which have been frozen for a number of years. Here's how it actually works, and what genuinely reduces a bill.

The nil-rate band and residence nil-rate band

Every estate benefits from a tax-free allowance called the nil-rate band, currently £325,000 per person — a threshold that has been frozen for a number of consecutive tax years, meaning more estates are drawn into Inheritance Tax over time simply through asset values (particularly property) rising. On top of this, the residence nil-rate band provides an additional allowance, currently up to £175,000, where a main residence is left to direct descendants — children, grandchildren, and certain others. Combined, a single person can potentially shield up to £500,000 from Inheritance Tax, and a married couple potentially up to £1 million between them, using both allowances together.

The residence nil-rate band tapers away for larger estates

For estates above a set value threshold (currently £2 million), the residence nil-rate band reduces gradually and can disappear entirely for very large estates — an important detail for anyone with a high-value estate to factor into planning.

The tax rate

The value of an estate above the available nil-rate band(s) is generally taxed at 40%. A reduced rate of 36% applies to the whole taxable estate where at least 10% of the net estate is left to charity — a meaningful incentive that's often overlooked in estate planning.

Transfers between spouses

Assets left to a UK-domiciled spouse or civil partner are generally exempt from Inheritance Tax entirely, regardless of value. Additionally, any portion of the nil-rate band and residence nil-rate band unused on the first spouse's death can typically be transferred to the surviving spouse, potentially doubling their own available allowances when they later die — this is a significant planning point for married couples and civil partners.

Gifts and the 7-year rule

Gifts made during someone's lifetime can reduce the value of their estate for Inheritance Tax purposes, but the timing matters. Gifts made more than 7 years before death are generally treated as fully outside the estate ("potentially exempt transfers"). Gifts made between 3 and 7 years before death benefit from taper relief, reducing the tax rate applied on a sliding scale. Certain gifts are exempt regardless of timing, including an annual gift allowance, wedding gifts up to set limits, and regular gifts made out of surplus income.

Other reliefs and exemptions

Beyond the core allowances, other reliefs can significantly reduce a bill in the right circumstances — including Business Relief (potentially reducing tax on qualifying business assets) and Agricultural Relief (for qualifying farmland and farm property). These reliefs have specific, sometimes complex qualifying conditions, so it's worth getting advice tailored to the actual assets involved rather than assuming a relief applies.

This guide describes the general Inheritance Tax framework in the UK as at the date above, based on thresholds and rates that have been frozen for several tax years — always check current figures, as this is a guide, not tax advice.
