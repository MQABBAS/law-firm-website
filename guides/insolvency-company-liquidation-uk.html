Company Insolvency and Liquidation in the UK

8 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales

Realising your company can't pay its bills is one of the most stressful moments in business — but it's also a point where the decisions you make, and how quickly you make them, matter enormously, both for the company's chances of survival and for your own personal position as a director.

When is a company insolvent?

Under the Insolvency Act 1986, a company is generally treated as insolvent if it meets either of two tests: the cash-flow test (it can't pay its debts as they fall due) or the balance-sheet test (its total liabilities exceed its total assets, including contingent and prospective liabilities). A company doesn't need to fail both tests — either one is enough to trigger insolvency, and the practical reality for most struggling businesses is the cash-flow test: bills mounting up that simply can't be paid on time.

Director duties when insolvency looms

The moment a company's directors know, or ought reasonably to conclude, that insolvent liquidation is likely, their legal duty shifts. Directors normally owe their duties primarily to the company and, through it, its shareholders — but once insolvency is likely, the interests of creditors come to the fore, and directors are expected to act in a way that minimises harm to creditors rather than, for example, continuing to run up further debt the company has no realistic hope of repaying.

Get advice as early as possible

The single biggest factor in outcomes for both companies and directors is how early proper advice is taken. Directors who seek guidance from an insolvency practitioner or solicitor at the first sign of serious cash-flow trouble have far more options — and far less personal risk — than those who wait until creditors are already at the door.

The main insolvency procedures

UK insolvency law offers several distinct routes, and which one fits depends heavily on whether the company (or its business) can realistically be saved:

  • Administration — a rescue-focused procedure. An administrator takes control of the company, and a statutory moratorium protects it from most creditor action (including winding-up petitions and most legal proceedings) while a rescue plan, sale of the business, or better-than-liquidation outcome is pursued.
  • Company Voluntary Arrangement (CVA) — a formal, court-free agreement with creditors to repay debts (often partially) over an agreed period, typically letting the company keep trading throughout.
  • Creditors' Voluntary Liquidation (CVL) — directors and shareholders choose to wind the company up when it's insolvent and there's no realistic prospect of rescue, appointing a liquidator to realise assets and distribute proceeds to creditors.
  • Compulsory liquidation — a creditor (or occasionally the company itself, a shareholder, or in some cases the Secretary of State) petitions the court to wind the company up, typically after a statutory demand or judgment debt goes unpaid.

Liquidation in more detail

Whichever route into liquidation is taken, the effect is broadly similar: a licensed insolvency practitioner is appointed as liquidator, the directors' powers cease, the company stops trading (save for anything needed to wind up its affairs), and the liquidator's job is to gather in and sell the company's assets, investigate its affairs (including directors' conduct), and distribute the proceeds to creditors in a set statutory order of priority — secured creditors, then certain preferential creditors (including some employee claims), then unsecured creditors, broadly pro rata. Shareholders are paid only if anything is left, which in an insolvent liquidation is rare.

Personal liability risks

Limited liability generally protects directors and shareholders from being personally responsible for company debts — that's the whole point of trading through a limited company. But it isn't absolute. A liquidator (or administrator) can pursue directors personally for things like:

  • Wrongful trading — continuing to trade and incur credit when there was no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvent liquidation, and the director didn't take every step to minimise loss to creditors.
  • Fraudulent trading — trading with intent to defraud creditors, a much higher bar involving dishonesty, and one that also carries potential criminal liability.
  • Misfeasance or breach of duty — misuse of company money or property, or other breaches of a director's statutory duties.
  • Personal guarantees — many directors of small companies personally guarantee loans, leases, or supplier credit, which survive the company's insolvency regardless of any wrongdoing.

Directors can also be disqualified from acting as a director for up to 15 years under the Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986 if their conduct in the run-up to insolvency is found to be unfit.
