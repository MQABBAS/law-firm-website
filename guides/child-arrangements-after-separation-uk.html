Child Arrangements After Separation: A UK Parents' Guide

8 min read Updated August 2026 England & Wales

When parents separate, deciding where children live and how they spend time with each parent is often the hardest part. UK law strongly encourages parents to agree these arrangements themselves, and only involves the court where that's genuinely not possible. Here's how the system works.

On this page

  1. Parental responsibility
  2. Agreeing arrangements without court
  3. Mediation and the MIAM requirement
  4. Applying for a Child Arrangements Order

Parental responsibility

Parental responsibility is the legal term for the rights, duties, and responsibilities a parent has for a child — including decisions about education, medical treatment, and religion. It's separate from where a child lives day to day. A child's mother has it automatically; a father has it automatically if married to the mother at birth or named on the birth certificate (for registrations after December 2003). Unmarried fathers not on the certificate can acquire parental responsibility by agreement with the mother or by court order.

Agreeing arrangements without court

Most separated parents agree living and contact arrangements between themselves, sometimes with the help of a solicitor to document the agreement (a "parenting plan"). This isn't legally binding in the way a court order is, but it avoids the cost, delay, and stress of court proceedings, and can be revisited as children grow and circumstances change.

Mediation and the MIAM requirement

If parents can't agree, the next step before court is usually family mediation — a neutral third party helps both sides work towards an agreement. In most cases, you're required to attend a Mediation Information and Assessment Meeting (MIAM) before applying to court, so you understand what mediation involves and whether it might work for your situation. Exemptions apply, notably in cases involving domestic abuse or safeguarding concerns, or genuine urgency.

Mediation isn't required in every case

If there's a history of domestic abuse, or a child is at risk of harm, the MIAM requirement can be waived. If you're in this situation, tell your solicitor early — different, more protective processes are available. See our guide on domestic abuse injunctions for related information.

Applying for a Child Arrangements Order

If agreement genuinely isn't possible, either parent can apply to the family court for a Child Arrangements Order, which can specify:

  • Who the child lives with (this can be one parent, or split between both — a "shared care" arrangement)
  • Who the child spends time with and how that contact happens (in person, supervised, remotely)

The court process typically involves an initial hearing, input from Cafcass (the Children and Family Court Advisory and Support Service) where relevant, and — if not resolved earlier — a final hearing where a judge makes a decision.

How the court decides

The child's welfare is the court's paramount consideration — not what's fair to either parent. Under the Children Act 1989 "welfare checklist," the court weighs factors including the child's own wishes and feelings (given their age and maturity), their physical, emotional and educational needs, the likely effect of any change in circumstances, and each parent's capacity to meet the child's needs. There's no automatic presumption that a child should live with one parent over another based on gender.

This guide describes the general framework for child arrangements in England & Wales as at the date above and is for general information only — it is not legal advice. Every family's situation is different, particularly where safeguarding concerns are involved, so please get in touch to discuss your specific circumstances.
